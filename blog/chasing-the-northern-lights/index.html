Chasing the northern lights: what nobody tells you

Honest advice about seeing the aurora: cloud, cold, patience, photography and why the experience is worth it even when the lights are faint.

By the Nexara Holidays travel desk · Updated 24 October 2026 · 2 min read

Seeing the northern lights is magical, but it is not like flicking a switch. Here is what to expect.

It depends on the weather

Clear skies matter as much as solar activity. Cloud is the most common reason people miss the aurora. Plan several nights in places like Tromsø, Rovaniemi (Lapland) or Iceland.

It can look different from photos

To the naked eye, a modest aurora can look like a pale green or grey glow. Cameras capture colours more strongly. Strong displays, though, are vivid and moving.

It is cold

Temperatures can be well below freezing. Dress in thermal layers and insulated outerwear; many tours provide thermal suits. See Packing for Europe in winter: staying warm without overpacking.

Patience is required

Displays can appear late at night and last minutes or hours. Guided tours chase clear skies and provide hot drinks and campfires.

Use forecasts

Aurora and cloud forecasts help, but they are not guarantees.

Photography tips

Use a tripod, a wide lens and long exposures, or a phone's night mode.

Plan other activities

Husky sledding, reindeer farms, snowmobiling, saunas and whale watching make the trip rewarding even on cloudy nights.

When to go

September to March. See Planning a northern lights trip: where, when and how many nights and our Festival and seasonal trips page.

Where to stay

Staying outside city lights improves your chances. Glass igloos and cabins in Lapland let you watch the sky from bed. In Tromsø, guided chases drive inland to find clear skies.

How long

Four to five nights gives you several chances. Combine the aurora with Christmas markets or a city break in Helsinki or Oslo.
